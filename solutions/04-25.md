**Central idea.** Both are unit-Jacobian shifts: $Y = X+Z$ gives $z = y-x$, and $Y = X^2+Z$ gives $z = y-x^2$; substitute into the joint pdf of $(X,Z)$ and read off the support.

**Solution.** In Example 4.5.8, $X \sim$ uniform$(0,1)$, $Z \sim$ uniform$(0,1/10)$ independent, so $f_{X,Z}(x,z) = 10$ on $(0,1)\times(0,1/10)$. With $Y = X+Z$, the transformation $(x,z)\mapsto(x,y)$ has $|J|=1$ ($z = y-x$), giving $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 10$ for $0<x<1$, $x<y<x+1/10$. In Example 4.5.9, $X \sim$ uniform$(-1,1)$, $Z \sim$ uniform$(0,1/10)$ independent, $f_{X,Z}(x,z) = 5$ on $(-1,1)\times(0,1/10)$. With $Y = X^2+Z$ ($z = y-x^2$, $|J|=1$), $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 5$ for $-1<x<1$, $x^2<y<x^2+1/10$.
