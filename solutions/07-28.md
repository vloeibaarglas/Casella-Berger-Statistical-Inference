**Central idea.** This is the Poisson model of Example 7.2.17 in action: the complete-data MLEs are ratios of sums, and each missing-data pattern deletes one term from one score equation.
**Solution.** Here $n=18$, $\sum x_i=55{,}860$, $\sum y_i=47$.
(a) Full data: $\hat\beta=\sum y_i/\sum x_i=47/55{,}860\approx 8.414\times10^{-4}$, $\hat\tau_j=(x_j+y_j)/(\hat\beta+1)$. With $x_1$ missing (Exercise 7.27(c)): $\hat\beta=\sum_{i=2}^{18}y_i/\sum_{i=2}^{18}x_i=44/52{,}320\approx 8.410\times10^{-4}$, $\hat\tau_1=y_1/\hat\beta\approx 3567.3$, $\hat\tau_j=(x_j+y_j)/(\hat\beta+1)$ for $j\ge 2$. The estimates are nearly identical to the full-data fit.
(b) With $y_1$ missing, the E-step imputes $E[Y_1\mid\cdot]=\beta^{(r)}\tau_1^{(r)}$ and the M-step gives $\beta^{(r+1)}=(E[Y_1]+\sum_{i\ge2}y_i)/\sum x_i$, $\tau_j^{(r+1)}=(x_j+\tilde y_j)/(\beta^{(r+1)}+1)$. Iterating to convergence: $\hat\beta=44/52{,}320\approx 8.410\times10^{-4}$ (same $\hat\beta$ as in (a)), $\hat\tau_1=x_1=3{,}540$ exactly, and $\hat\tau_j=(x_j+y_j)/(\hat\beta+1)$ for $j\ge2$ — again essentially the full-data fit, as expected since only one count of 47 is missing.
