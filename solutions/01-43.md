**Central idea.** Truncating the alternating inclusion–exclusion sum gives alternating upper/lower bounds (Bonferroni); the remainder has sign $(-1)^m$ by the binomial tail identity. *Caveat on (b): as stated it is not correct in general — see below.*

**Solution.** (a) With $S_m = P_1 - P_2 + \cdots \pm P_m$, Boole's inequality is $P(\cup_{i=1}^n A_i) \leq P_1 = S_1$ (Theorem 1.2.11), and $P(\cup_{i=1}^n A_i) \geq P_1 - P_2 = S_2$; both are the $m=1,2$ cases of the alternating bounds below. The book's Bonferroni inequality (1.2.10) is the intersection form: applying Boole to complements, $P(\cap_{i=1}^n A_i) = 1-P(\cup_{i=1}^n A_i^c) \geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i^c) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A_i)-(n-1)$.
For the bounds: from 1.42, writing $P_j = \sum_{k \geq j}\binom{k}{j}P(E_k)$,
$$P(\cup_{i=1}^n A_i) - S_m = (-1)^m\sum_{k>m}P(E_k)\binom{k-1}{m},$$
since $1 - \sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\binom{k}{j} = (-1)^m\binom{k-1}{m}$ (for $k > m$; it is $0$ for $k \leq m$). The sum is nonnegative, so the error has sign $(-1)^m$: odd partial sums are upper bounds, even ones lower bounds.
(b) *As stated this is not true in general — neither direction holds.* Counterexample to the exercise's direction: disjoint $A_i$ with $P(A_i) > 0$ gives $P_1 > 0 = P_2$. Counterexample to the reverse direction: $A_1 = \cdots = A_n = S$ with $n \geq 4$ gives $P_2 = \binom{n}{2} > n = P_1$. What *is* true and relevant: the *normalized* averages $P_k/\binom{n}{k}$ are decreasing in $k$ (each $k$-fold intersection contains the $(k+1)$-fold ones: $\binom{l}{k}P_l \leq \binom{n-k}{\,l-k\,}P_k$ for $k<l$). The alternating bounds above are valid regardless; they "improve" in the typical (rare-event) applications, but not universally — see (c).
(c) If $A_i = A$ for all $i$ with $P(A) = p$, then $P_k = \binom{n}{k}p$ and $S_m = p\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\binom{n}{j}$. These oscillate with *growing* amplitude (e.g., $n=10$: $S_1 = 10p$, $S_2 = -35p$, $S_3 = 85p$, …), since the binomial coefficients peak mid-sum. The bounds remain valid ($S_{\text{odd}} \geq p \geq S_{\text{even}}$) but are useless until nearly all $n$ terms are included — the extreme illustration of Schwager's caution about highly correlated events.
