**Central idea.** This is polar coordinates in disguise ($Y_1 = R^2$, $Y_2 = \cos\Theta$); the transformation is two-to-one (sign of $X_2$), and the joint density factors into a radial piece times an angular piece.

**Solution.** (a) Write $X_1 = Y_2\sqrt{Y_1}$, $X_2 = \pm\sqrt{Y_1(1-Y_2^2)}$; each branch has Jacobian $|J| = 1/(2\sqrt{1-Y_2^2})$. Summing both branches,
$$f_{Y_1,Y_2}(y_1,y_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2\sqrt{1-y_2^2}}e^{-y_1/(2\sigma^2)},\quad y_1 > 0,\ |y_2| < 1.$$
(b) This factors as $\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}e^{-y_1/(2\sigma^2)}\right]\left[\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-y_2^2}}\right]$: $Y_1 \sim \text{exponential}(2\sigma^2)$ (i.e. $\text{gamma}(1,2\sigma^2)$ with scale $2\sigma^2$) independent of $Y_2$, which has the arcsine distribution on $(-1,1)$. Geometrically, $Y_1 = R^2$ is the squared radius and $Y_2 = \cos\Theta$ encodes the angle; for iid normals the radius is independent of the direction.
