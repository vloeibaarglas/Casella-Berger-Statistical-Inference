**Central idea.** Write the variance ratio as $\sum b_iq_i$; the constraints $\sum q_i=\sum q_i/b_i=1$ force the worst case onto two extreme $b$'s, and the resulting quadratic in $b_{\min}$ has zero discriminant.
**Solution.** (a) Since $1/\sigma_i^2=q_i^*/\mathrm{Var}(W^*)$, $\mathrm{Var}(W)=\sum q_i^2\sigma_i^2=\mathrm{Var}(W^*)\sum b_iq_i$ with $b_i=q_i/q_i^*$; also $\sum q_i/b_i=\sum q_i^*=1$. Maximizing $\sum b_iq_i$ under $\sum q_i=\sum q_i/b_i=1$, $q_i\ge0$: extreme points use at most two $b$'s, say $b_i<b_j$, giving value $b_i+b_j-b_ib_j=1-(1-b_i)(1-b_j)$. Since $\sum b_iq_i^*=1$ forces $b_{\min}\le1\le b_{\max}$, the maximum over $[b_{\min},b_{\max}]^2$ is $b_{\min}+b_{\max}-b_{\min}b_{\max}$. With $r=b_{\max}/b_{\min}=(1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)$, $\frac{1}{1-\lambda^2}=\frac{(r+1)^2}{4r}$, and $\frac{(r+1)^2}{4r}-(b_{\min}+rb_{\min}-rb_{\min}^2)=r\big(b_{\min}-\frac{r+1}{2r}\big)^2\ge0$. Hence $\mathrm{Var}(W)/\mathrm{Var}(W^*)\le1/(1-\lambda^2)$.
(b) For $q_i=1/k$, $b_i=(1/k)/q_i^*$ so $b_{\max}/b_{\min}=\sigma_{\max}^2/\sigma_{\min}^2=:r$. Thus $\mathrm{Var}(\bar W)/\mathrm{Var}(W^*)\le(r+1)^2/(4r)$: the ordinary mean is at most $(r+1)^2/(4r)$ times worse than optimal (equality only when the variances take two values in the right proportion); it is near-optimal when $\sigma_{\max}^2/\sigma_{\min}^2\approx1$ and can be arbitrarily bad when the ratio is large.
