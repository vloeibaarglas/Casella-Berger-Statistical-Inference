**Central idea.** It is the discrete Stein identity $E[(r+X)(1-p)g(X+1)]=E[Xg(X)]$: shift the summation index and use $(r+y-1)\binom{r+y-2}{y-1}=y\binom{r+y-1}{y}$ to absorb the weight into the pmf.

**Solution.** Theorem 3.6.8(b): for $X\sim\text{negative binomial}(r,p)$ and bounded $g$,
$$E\big[(r+X)(1-p)\,g(X+1)\big] = E\big[X\,g(X)\big].$$
Indeed,
$$E[(r+X)(1-p)g(X+1)]=\sum_{x=0}^\infty(r+x)(1-p)g(x+1)\binom{r+x-1}{x}p^r(1-p)^x.$$
Put $y=x+1$: $=\sum_{y=1}^\infty(r+y-1)(1-p)g(y)\binom{r+y-2}{y-1}p^r(1-p)^{y-1}$. Since $(r+y-1)\binom{r+y-2}{y-1}=\frac{(r+y-1)!}{(y-1)!\,(r-1)!}=y\binom{r+y-1}{y}$, this equals $\sum_{y=1}^\infty y\,g(y)\binom{r+y-1}{y}p^r(1-p)^y=\sum_{y\ge1}y\,g(y)p(y)=E[Xg(X)]$ (the $y=0$ term is 0).
