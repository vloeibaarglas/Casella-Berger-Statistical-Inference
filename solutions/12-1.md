**Central idea.** The closest point on the line to $(x',y')$ is the one for which the displacement vector is orthogonal to the line's direction — equivalently, the point that makes the Pythagorean decomposition of squared distances hold for every other point on the line.

**Solution.** Parametrize points on $y=a+bx$ as $(u,a+bu)$. The squared distance from $(x',y')$ is $D(u)=(x'-u)^2+(y'-a-bu)^2$. By Pythagoras, $(u,a+bu)$ is the closest point iff the displacement $(x'-u,\;y'-a-bu)$ is orthogonal to the line's direction $(1,b)$ for every competing point, i.e. $(x'-u,y'-a-bu)\cdot(1,b)=0$. This gives $(x'-u)+b(y'-a-bu)=0$, whence $u(1+b^2)=x'+b(y'-a)$, i.e.
$$\hat x'=\frac{by'+x'-ab}{1+b^2},\qquad \hat y'=a+b\hat x'=a+\frac{b}{1+b^2}(by'+x'-ab),$$
which is (12.2.7). (Direct minimization of $D(u)$ gives the same first-order condition.)
