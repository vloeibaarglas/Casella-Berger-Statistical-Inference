**Central idea.** $|X| \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)$ carries all the $\theta$-information (the sign is a fair coin flip); the exponential representation breaks at the endpoints $\theta = 0,1$.

**Solution.** Here $P_\theta(X = 0) = 1-\theta$, $P_\theta(X = \pm 1) = \theta/2$.
(a) $X$ is sufficient (the whole sample always is). $\mathrm{E}_\theta[g(X)] = g(0) + \theta\big[\frac{g(-1)+g(1)}{2}-g(0)\big] \equiv 0$ on $[0,1]$ gives $g(0) = 0$, $g(-1)+g(1) = 0$, with nonzero solutions (e.g. $g(-1) = 1$, $g(1) = -1$, $g(0) = 0$). Not complete.
(b) $|X|$ takes values $0,1$ with $P_\theta(|X| = 1) = \theta$: given $|X|$, $X$ is $0$ or $\pm 1$ with probability $1/2$ each, free of $\theta$, so $|X|$ is sufficient. $\mathrm{E}_\theta[g(|X|)] = g(0) + \theta[g(1)-g(0)] \equiv 0$ forces $g(0) = g(1) = 0$: complete. Yes.
(c) No. On $0 < \theta < 1$ one can write $f(x\mid\theta) = (1-\theta)\exp\big(|x|\log\frac{\theta}{2(1-\theta)}\big)$, but no representation $h(x)c(\theta)e^{w(\theta)|x|}$ works on all of $[0,1]$: at $\theta = 0$, $f(0\mid 0) = 1$ forces $c(0),h(0) > 0$, while $f(1\mid 0) = 0$ then forces $h(1) = 0$, contradicting $f(1\mid\theta) = \theta/2 > 0$ for $\theta \in (0,1)$.
