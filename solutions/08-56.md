**Central idea.** Test I rejects for small $X$ — evidence *for* $H_0$ — so its risk is perverse on $H_0$; but on $H_1$ just above $1/3$ it is Test I that has the smaller risk. Neither test dominates the other.

**Solution.** Under 0–1 loss, $R(p) = P_p(\text{reject})$ on $H_0$ ($p \le 1/3$) and $1-P_p(\text{reject})$ on $H_1$. $P_p(\text{I rejects}) = P_p(X \le 1) = (1-p)^4(1+4p)$; $P_p(\text{II rejects}) = P_p(X \ge 4) = p^4(5-4p)$.

![Risk functions of Test I and Test II under 0–1 loss.]({{ '/plots/ex856_risk.png' | relative_url }})

On $H_0$, Test II is far better: at $p = 1/3$, Test I rejects with probability $(2/3)^4(7/3) \approx .46$ versus $\approx .045$ for Test II. But on $H_1$ just above $1/3$, Test I has the *smaller* risk (at $p = .35$: $.572$ vs $.946$; at $p = .4$: $.663$ vs $.913$); the risks are equal at $p = 1/2$ ($.8125$ each), and only for large $p$ does Test II win decisively (risk $\to 0$ at $p = 1$ while Test I's stays near $1$). So neither test uniformly dominates — Test I is not inadmissible, it is simply perverse on $H_0$ and competitive on $H_1$ near the boundary.
