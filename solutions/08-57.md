**Central idea.** The interval test $\delta$ wastes rejection probability where it hurts (under $H_0$) and misses it where it helps (under $H_1$); the monotone test $\delta_c$ with $c \approx 1.10$ (matched to $\delta$'s risk at $\mu = 0$) dominates it, as MLR theory predicts.

**Solution.** Under 0–1 loss, $R(\mu,\delta) = \Phi(2-\mu)-\Phi(1-\mu)$ for $\mu \le 0$ and $1-\Phi(2-\mu)+\Phi(1-\mu)$ for $\mu > 0$; $R(\mu,\delta_c) = 1-\Phi(c-\mu)$ for $\mu \le 0$ and $\Phi(c-\mu)$ for $\mu > 0$. Matching risks at the boundary $\mu = 0$ ($1-\Phi(c) = \Phi(2)-\Phi(1)$) gives $c = \Phi^{-1}(1-\Phi(2)+\Phi(1)) \approx 1.10$. Then $R(\mu,\delta_c) \le R(\mu,\delta)$ for all $\mu$ (verified numerically; equality only at $\mu = 0$):

![Risk functions of $\delta$ (reject $1<X<2$) and $\delta_c$ (reject $X>1.10$).]({{ '/plots/ex857_risk.png' | relative_url }})

$\delta_c$ shifts rejection mass from the interval $(1,2)$ — which under $H_0$ ($\mu \le 0$) only creates Type I error — out to $(c,\infty)$, where it buys power under $H_1$; by the MLR of $n(\mu,1)$ in $X$, the monotone test is uniformly better.
