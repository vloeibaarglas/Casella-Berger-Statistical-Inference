**Central idea.** For bounded odd scores with nonzero opposite limits, the displayed formula gives $\eta=1$, $b^*=1/2$; the normal (mean) $\psi$ is unbounded, hence $b^*=0$. AREs come from (10.2.9) by numerical integration.

**Solution.** (a) (i) Normal: $\psi(x)=x$ unbounded — the formula does not apply; $b^*=0$ (the mean). (ii) Logistic $\psi=\tanh$: $\psi(\pm\infty)=\pm1$, $\eta=\min\{1,1\}=1$, $b^*=1/2$. (iii) Cauchy $\psi=2x/(1+x^2)$ tends to zero at both infinities, so the displayed expression for $\eta$ is $0/0$ and cannot be applied. A finite-$M$ ratio does not repair that theorem; breakdown must be proved for the specified estimator and rule for selecting a root or objective minimum. (iv) Least informative: $\psi(\pm\infty)=\pm c$, $\eta=1$, $b^*=1/2$. Use the corrected logistic score $\tanh(x/2)$ from 10.29. The score choices are efficient for their respective models; logistic and least-informative scores satisfy the stated nonzero-limit breakdown calculation. The Cauchy score is bounded, but its breakdown conclusion needs a separate argument and an estimator-selection rule.
(b) (i) Huber $\psi$: bounded with $\psi(\pm\infty)=\pm k$: $\eta=1$, $b^*=1/2$. (ii) Tukey and (iii) Andrews scores vanish identically sufficiently far out, so both give an undefined $0/0$ in the displayed formula. Their estimating equations may have multiple roots. Boundedness and oddness alone do not justify a universal breakdown value of $1/2$; specify scale estimation and root/global-minimum selection before making that claim.
(c) From (10.2.9) (larger = better, max 1), with the following fixed tuning constants (the printed unsquared Tukey score is not the usual squared biweight), using a scale-one Laplace error law (Huber $k=1.345$, Tukey $c=4.685$, Andrews $c=1.339$):

| | Huber | Tukey | Andrews |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 0.95 | 0.98 | 0.95 |
| Double exp. | 0.70 | 0.65 | 0.70 |

The normal efficiencies are approximately $0.95000$, $0.98358$, and $0.95004$. These efficiency calculations do not establish breakdown for the redescending scores; for double-exponential data (where the MLE is the median) they keep 65–70% efficiency — the robustness–efficiency compromise.
