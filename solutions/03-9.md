**Central idea.** One school is a Binomial$(60,1/90)$ tail event — rare but not absurd; multiply the opportunities (schools $\times$ counties $\times$ states $\times$ years) and the rarity evaporates: the law of truly large numbers.

**Solution.** (a) Treat each of the 60 entering children as an (approximately) independent twin-birth trial with $p = 1/90$; five sets of twins $\approx$ 5 or more successes. $P(X \ge 5) = \sum_{k=5}^{60}\binom{60}{k}(1/90)^k(89/90)^{60-k} \approx 5.6\times10^{-4} \approx 1/1800$ (Poisson$(2/3)$ approximation: $6.3\times10^{-4}$). For one pre-specified school this is rare — arguably newsworthy locally — but not a "statistical impossibility." (b) With $62 \times 5 = 310$ schools, $P(\text{some school has } \ge 5) = 1-(1-p)^{310} \approx 0.16$. About a 1-in-6 chance somewhere in the state — hardly impossible. (c) With $310 \times 50 \times 10 = 155{,}000$ school-years, $1-(1-p)^{155000} \approx 1-e^{-86} \approx 1$: virtually certain to occur somewhere. So no — it is exactly the law of truly large numbers: with enough opportunities, even 1-in-1800 events are expected to happen.
