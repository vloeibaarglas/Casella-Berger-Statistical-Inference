**Central idea.** An unordered sample has multinomial multiplicity. Summing multiplicities counts ordered samples; summing ones counts unordered samples.

**Solution.** (a) The six permutations of $(4,4,12,12)$ are $(4,4,12,12)$, $(4,12,4,12)$, $(4,12,12,4)$, $(12,4,4,12)$, $(12,4,12,4)$, and $(12,12,4,4)$. The twelve permutations of $(2,9,9,12)$ are
$$\begin{gathered}(2,9,9,12),(2,9,12,9),(2,12,9,9),\\(9,2,9,12),(9,2,12,9),(9,9,2,12),(9,9,12,2),\\(9,12,2,9),(9,12,9,2),\\(12,2,9,9),(12,9,2,9),(12,9,9,2).\end{gathered}$$
(b) There are $6!/(1!2!1!2!)=180$ favorable ordered samples out of $6^6$, giving $5/1296$.
(c) Choose positions for each repeated value: successive binomial coefficients multiply to $k!/(k_1!\cdots k_m!)$.
(d) As printed, the identity is false: the weighted sum equals $m^k$ by the multinomial theorem, not $\binom{k+m-1}{k}$ (e.g. $m=k=2$ gives $1+2+1=4\ne3$). Stars and bars counts the *summands*: $\sum_{k_1,\ldots,k_m\ge0}1\{k_1+\cdots+k_m=k\}=\binom{k+m-1}{k}$, by placing $m-1$ separators among $k$ identical objects.
