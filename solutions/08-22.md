**Central idea.** (a) The NP ratio $(3/2)^{10}(1/3)^S$ is decreasing in $S$, and $P_{1/2}(S\le2)=56/1024\approx.0547$ is exactly attainable. (c) Simple vs.\ simple: Neyman–Pearson delivers a UMP test for every $\alpha\in[0,1]$.

**Solution.** Let $S=\sum X_i \sim \text{binomial}(10,p)$.
(a) $\frac{L(1/4)}{L(1/2)} = (\frac{1/4}{1/2})^S(\frac{3/4}{1/2})^{10-S} = (3/2)^{10}(1/3)^S$, strictly decreasing in $S$; reject iff $S \le c$. $P_{1/2}(S\le1)=11/1024\approx.0107$, $P_{1/2}(S\le2)=56/1024\approx.05469$: so $c=2$ gives size exactly $.0547$. MP test: reject iff $S\le2$. Power $= P_{1/4}(S\le2) = \sum_{k=0}^2\binom{10}{k}(1/4)^k(3/4)^{10-k} \approx .0563+.1877+.2816 \approx .526$.
(b) $\gamma(p) = P_p(S\ge6) = \sum_{k=6}^{10}\binom{10}{k}p^k(1-p)^{10-k}$, strictly increasing in $p$ (MLR). Size $= \sup_{p\le1/2}\gamma(p) = \gamma(1/2) = 1-\frac{638}{1024} = \frac{386}{1024} \approx .377$. The power curve rises from $0$ (at $p=0$) through $.377$ at $p=1/2$ to $1$ (at $p=1$), steepest near $p=1/2$.
(c) All of them: for simple $H_0$ vs.\ simple $H_1$ the Neyman–Pearson test is UMP for every $\alpha \in [0,1]$ (randomization on the boundary is used when $\alpha$ is not a binomial tail probability, as it is here).
