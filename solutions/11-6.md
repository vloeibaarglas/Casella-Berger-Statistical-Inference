**Central idea.** The normal likelihood factors through the cell means and within-cell sums of squares; independence across cells plus Lemma 5.3.3 ($\bar X \perp S^2$ for normal samples) gives (b); with $\sigma^2$ known the standardized cell means are exactly the canonical $n(\eta_i,1)$ model.

**Solution.** (a) With $Y_{ij} \sim n(\theta_i,\sigma^2)$ independent, the likelihood is $\prod_i (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n_i/2}\exp(-\sum_j(y_{ij}-\theta_i)^2/(2\sigma^2))$. Since $\sum_j(y_{ij}-\theta_i)^2 = \sum_j(y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot})^2 + n_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\theta_i)^2 = (n_i-1)s_i^2 + n_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\theta_i)^2$, the likelihood depends on the data only through the $\bar y_{i\cdot}$ and $s_i^2$ (equivalently $S_p^2$), so these are sufficient by the Factorization Theorem.
(b) The $k$ samples are independent across $i$. By Lemma 5.3.3, for a normal sample $\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} \perp S_i^2$; hence each $S_i^2$ is independent of all $\bar{Y}_{j\cdot}$, and so is $S_p^2$, a function of the $S_i^2$ only.
(c) With $\sigma^2$ known, the $\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} \sim n(\theta_i, \sigma^2/n_i)$ are sufficient for the $\theta_i$. Standardizing, $X_i = \sqrt{n_i}\,\bar{Y}_{i\cdot}/\sigma \sim n(\eta_i, 1)$ independently with $\eta_i = \sqrt{n_i}\,\theta_i/\sigma$ — precisely the canonical model of Miscellanea 11.5.6 (independent unit-variance normals). Inference on $\theta_i$ is equivalent to inference on $\eta_i$.
